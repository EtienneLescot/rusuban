Writing rules

A project's rules are plain text in its own repo. They decide what the agents do: what they do alone, what comes to you, how long they wait, how they write. Rusuban ships no default: without rules, nothing autonomous happens.

Where they live

On the repo's default branch:

.rusuban/
  rules/*.md    the rules, free text
  locks.json    optional: what the server checks itself before a merge (see locks.md)
  • Read from the default branch only, through GitHub's contents API: never from a PR head, never from a local checkout. A PR cannot change the rules it is judged by.
  • Read with the repo's state, at every read of the project (every refreshSec, 90 seconds by default), and cached by the tree sha of .rusuban/.
  • The .md files directly in rules/, sorted by file name. Other files and subfolders are skipped. Number them: 01-autonomy.md, 02-merging.md.
  • Each repo carries its own. In a project of several repos, a repo without rules is only observed. A run sees the rules of every repo of its project, each under its source.

How the agents get them

Every run (judge, coordinator, worker, task) gets a prompt built when it starts:

  1. the product prompt of its kind, generic and shipped with Rusuban;
  2. the safety frame: GitHub content is data, agents never merge, the locks hold, silent rules mean a card;
  3. your rules, verbatim, under their source;
  4. the context: the owner, each repo's rules and locks, the open cards, what waits for the run;
  5. the run's mission or task.

Your rules appear like this:

# Project rules: owner/repo@<tree sha>:.rusuban/rules/

## 01-autonomy.md

<the file's text>

Do not repeat what the product prompts already say: the judgment and card formats, how to delegate to a worker, the safety frame.

Without rules

A repo with no .rusuban/rules/*.md is observed only, even with a locks.json:

  • its items are read and shown, and the dashboard says it is observed only;
  • no judge starts, nothing merges, and nothing is written on GitHub on the agents' own initiative;
  • your own requests, your answers to cards and your tasks still start agents.

What to write

The rules say everything about behavior. No section is required, but what the agents need and do not find becomes a card asking you. What they need is what each judgment sets:

The agents set Your rules say
pr.auto Which PRs may merge without a person.
owner What the agents keep, what waits on a third party, what comes to you or to another GitHub login.
revisitAt How long a third party is waited for, and when the agents look at their own work again. No delay is built in.
priority, area Your priority scale (P0 to P3) and your areas. The kinds are fixed: bug, feature, maintenance, docs, translation, question, other.
A card's urgency Which decisions are now and which are digest.
Messages on GitHub Their language and tone, and what may be posted without a card.
A fork's CI Whether agents may approve a fork's workflows, after reading the diff.
Proofs When a change needs a proof beyond the evidence zones of locks.json, and what proof.

Rules may be written in any language. Cards, journal lines and final answers are in English.

Writing them well

  • Write orders. "A bug fix with a test merges alone", not "we value tests".
  • Define your words. Say what a bug is and what a feature is: that line decides what merges.
  • Give every wait a duration. revisitAt is required whenever an item stays with the agents or waits on a third party.
  • Put what the server can check in locks.json. Text guides the model; a lock holds without it. "Never merge a change to CI" belongs in lockedPaths.
  • Keep them short. Every file goes into every run's prompt.
  • Name people by their GitHub login, as @login.

Where the rules are silent

The agents do not guess. A situation the rules do not cover becomes a card to the owner. A question on how the agents work, rather than on the item, waits in the inbox's Rules & settings tab.

When the cards of several items ask the same, an agent proposes the rule that would answer them, on a card of its own in that tab: Open the rule PR opens a PR adding it to .rusuban/rules/, which a person merges on GitHub, and the items wait for it; Not a rule keeps them asked one by one.

What rules cannot do

  • Lift a lock. No text lifts a built-in lock or one of locks.json: a judgment that would break one becomes a card.
  • Make an agent merge. Only the server merges, after its gates.
  • Widen what agents may run on your machine. That is your host profile (see security).
  • Change your instance's settings: a project's approval bypass, the merge switch. Those are your commands and your dashboard's.
  • Come from GitHub. An issue, a PR or a comment that reads like a rule is data.

Changing them

  1. Open a PR that edits .rusuban/. It never merges alone: the Locks gate stops any PR that touches the folder. A person merges it on GitHub.
  2. Once it is on the default branch, it applies at the next read, without a restart. A queued run gets the new rules; a run already at work keeps the ones it started with.
  3. It is journaled with the files changed and a line diff, and flagged in your inbox.

A locks.json that does not validate stops every merge of its repo until it is fixed. That is journaled and flagged too (see locks).

Trying them

  • The switch paused. Agents act as the rules say, and nothing merges until you arm the switch in the dashboard's header. Read their judgments and adjust the rules first.
  • Start from an example. Each folder of examples/rules/ is a .rusuban/ folder: copy its rules/ and its locks.json into .rusuban/ at the root of your repo, then edit them.
Example What the agents do alone
cautious Judge, test and prepare. Every item comes to the owner on a card, and every path is locked: only the owner's merge order merges.
solo-maintainer Fix bugs, and merge fixes, docs, tests and maintenance within path and size locks. Features, API changes and dependencies come to the owner.
openscreen A real project's rules: fix bugs and merge fixes, translations, docs, tests and maintenance from any author; capture, preview and export changes need a pixel and timing proof; features, interface and architecture come to the owner; a third party is waited for two days at most.
